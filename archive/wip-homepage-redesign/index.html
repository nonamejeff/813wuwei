Tampa Bay Field Notes

813 Naturalist Guide

Tampa Bay is a subtropical coastal landscape shaped by fire, water, limestone-karst geology, and the pulse of seasonal rainfall.

Ecological Communities & Habitats of the Region

These habitats form a mosaic across uplands, wetlands, and tidal shorelines. Each is defined by soil, hydrology, fire, and salinity.

Pine Flatwoods

Flat, sandy to poorly drained soils with a high water table. A history of frequent lightning-driven fires keeps an open pine canopy and a dense, fire-adapted understory. Seasonal wet and dry swings drive the system.

Representative native plants

  • Slash pine — Pinus elliottii
  • Longleaf pine (where present) — Pinus palustris
  • Saw palmetto — Serenoa repens
  • Gallberry — Ilex glabra
  • Wiregrass — Aristida stricta (or locally Aristida beyrichiana)
  • Fetterbush — Lyonia lucida

Oak Hammocks

Upland hardwood forests on slightly higher, better-drained ground. Fire is less frequent than in flatwoods, so the canopy closes and creates a cool, buffered interior. Often occurs as islands of hardwoods embedded in pine systems.

Representative native plants

  • Live oak — Quercus virginiana
  • Laurel oak — Quercus laurifolia
  • Cabbage palm — Sabal palmetto
  • Beautyberry — Callicarpa americana
  • Yaupon holly — Ilex vomitoria
  • American elm (in mesic hammocks) — Ulmus americana

Sandhill

Deep, well-drained sands with a low water table and very frequent fire. Widely spaced pines and an open, grassy ground layer dominate until fire is excluded and oaks and shrubs fill in.

Representative native plants

  • Longleaf pine — Pinus palustris
  • Turkey oak — Quercus laevis
  • Wiregrass — Aristida stricta (or A. beyrichiana)
  • Gopher apple — Licania michauxii
  • Prickly pear — Opuntia humifusa
  • Chapman’s oak (local) — Quercus chapmanii

Freshwater Marshes & Wet Prairies

Shallow, seasonally or permanently inundated wetlands dominated by grasses, sedges, and emergent plants. Hydroperiod and water depth determine plant communities as wet-season expansion and dry-season drawdowns reset the system.

Representative native plants

  • Maidencane — Panicum hemitomon
  • Pickerelweed — Pontederia cordata
  • Duck potato — Sagittaria lancifolia
  • Soft rush — Juncus effusus
  • Southern cattail — Typha domingensis
  • Sawgrass (in appropriate settings) — Cladium jamaicense

Mangrove Forests

Tidal coastal forests tolerant of salt and flooding. Their root structures stabilize shorelines, trap sediment, and create nursery habitat. Zonation reflects salinity and flooding frequency.

Representative native plants

  • Red mangrove — Rhizophora mangle
  • Black mangrove — Avicennia germinans
  • White mangrove — Laguncularia racemosa
  • Buttonwood — Conocarpus erectus

Salt Marshes

Tidal herbaceous wetlands on protected shorelines and estuaries, shaped by salinity gradients and tidal inundation. Marsh plants are adapted to salt stress and fluctuating oxygen in saturated soils.

Representative native plants

  • Smooth cordgrass — Spartina alterniflora
  • Saltwort — Batis maritima
  • Glasswort — Salicornia spp.
  • Sea oxeye daisy — Borrichia frutescens
  • Marsh elder — Iva frutescens

Riverine / Floodplain Forests

Forests along rivers and streams with periodic flooding and nutrient-rich alluvial soils. Plant communities shift with elevation from frequently flooded banks to higher natural levees, while deadwood, flood pulses, and shade create complexity.

Representative native plants

  • Bald cypress — Taxodium distichum
  • Tupelo / blackgum — Nyssa biflora (and related Nyssa spp.)
  • Red maple — Acer rubrum
  • River birch — Betula nigra
  • Swamp bay — Persea palustris
  • Buttonbush — Cephalanthus occidentalis

Why These Systems Matter

  • Fire ecology maintains open uplands and plant diversity.
  • Seasonal flooding drives wetland productivity and wildlife use.
  • Mangroves and marshes buffer storm surge and reduce shoreline erosion.
  • Habitat diversity concentrates biodiversity across a compact region.

Local Context

These habitats can be observed across Tampa Bay uplands, river corridors, and coastal edges. The guide will expand into habitat pages and species profiles to support field exploration and conservation.
